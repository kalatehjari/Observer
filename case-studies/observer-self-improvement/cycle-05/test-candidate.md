# Observer analysis: how to revise "Graded Authority for AI over Sensitive Human Matters"

*Observer multi-lens reasoning at full depth (high-stakes question). Material: the supplied paper. Analysis date: 22 October 2026.*

## Summary

**Recommendation.** Keep the paper's thesis and its five-grade architecture, but do not circulate it until four things are done: (1) define "final authority" and repair the tables and cross-references; (2) make grade 1 a tested grade, because every adverse determination sits there; (3) confine the asymmetry premise and the escalation carve-out to decisions that take nothing from others, and give ceilings a route upward as well as down; (4) argue the military line and the hardest objections from the evidence and law that bear on them, and correct the overstated claims. Then re-audit and pilot the tables. If the author wants to keep the claim that the asymmetry premise "governs direction", it needs evidence of the error ordering in at least one domain; otherwise it should become a conditional rule inside Section 5.

## 2. Recommendation

1. Define "final authority" in §2 as settling a person's outcome at grade 4, and restate the thesis in two limbs: no grade 4 for any decision about a person; adverse, value-laden and force-against-persons determinations at grade 1, with the narrow statutory exception for credit refusals. (G2, G21)
2. In the same edit, regrade the protective stop to 3 and delete "with one exception", so the first limb holds without exception. (C3)
3. Make grade 1 testable: a class counts as grade 1 only if its deciders pass the real-override test, otherwise it is treated as grade 2; apply the triggers to grade-1 adverse classes. (G1, C6, C16)
4. Confine R2's statutory route to credit refusals, as Table 1 does, and show the credit ceiling as conditional. (C2, C12)
5. Resolve approvals by letting §6.4's conditions govern grade 2 (Option A). Treating high-volume approvals as grade-3 envelopes (Option B) is a values choice that needs evidence. (C1)
6. Confine the asymmetry premise to non-rivalrous decisions, replace "reversed" with *lapses*, name who checks the ordering and on what evidence, and fix §6.5's third-party list. (G3, C5, G15, C18)
7. Confine the escalation carve-out to protective flags that do not displace others, treat escalation to investigation as adverse, and add a displacement trigger. (W6)
8. Repair monitoring: triggers for the label loop and detection delay, a seeded-error catch-rate trigger, synthetic seeded cases, independent checks on real override, a named re-certifier and a suspension rule. (G10, G16, G8, G9, G17)
9. Markets: add traceability to the answerability test, narrow the "no one can answer" claims, define "market interventions" and align R6 with Table 1. (C4, W2, G12, C9)
10. Military: classify the §3.2 case, state why dosing differs from force, add the omitted ICRC limits and a rule for possibly crewed tracks, align R5, and answer the US standard. (G4, G5, C8, G14)
11. Add criteria for raising a ceiling, decide the low-resource baseline explicitly, and fix §6.6's proportionality and fallback sentences. (G6, G7, C6, C7)
12. Correct the overstated claims and engage the law on point: GDPR Article 22, the SCHUFA judgment, AI Act Article 14(4) and the US adverse-action reasons rule. (W1, W3, W4, W5, W7, W8, W9, G13)
13. Make the tests usable: show how each condition applies at each grade, replace "without further philosophical work", and align the conclusion's one test with the five. (G11, C10, C11)
14. Housekeeping. (C13, C14, C15, C17, G18, G19, G20, V1–V4)
15. Re-audit the revised text, because revisions often introduce new conflicts; then pilot Tables 1–2 on two or three real deployments with domain reviewers.

### What would change the recommendation

- Field evidence that grade-1 deciders reliably catch seeded errors in these domains would turn step 3 from a precondition into a monitoring duty.
- If the author wants a short position paper rather than a usable framework, dropping the tables removes most conflicts, and steps 3, 8 and 13 shrink to principles.
- Evidence of the error ordering in a domain would let the asymmetry premise keep a governing role there (D2).
- New law, such as an autonomous-weapons instrument or the final text of the AI Act omnibus, would change steps 10 and 12.
- If the paper's target settings are non-rivalrous (for example, consumer credit with ample capital), step 6 changes fewer rows.

## 4. Audit of the paper

Scope: the whole paper, that is the Abstract, §§1–8, Tables 1–2, R1–R8 and the references. Quotations are copied exactly; quotations from the tables copy the Markdown row text, pipe separators included. Each finding gives its locations and quotations, the problem, what it weakens, its severity, the smallest fix and any other passage that fix must change.

### 4.1 Consistency

**C1. Grade 2 for favourable approvals contradicts §6.4.** Conflict. **High.**
- §5: "Grade 2 suits favourable or cheaply corrected outcomes, or review that fits the workflow."
- §6.4: "Grade 2 is permissible only where review fits the workflow, overrides are logged, reviewers are tested with seeded errors, and the drift triggers in Section 5 are monitored."
- §6.4: "In high-volume settings where meaningful review is impossible, grade 2 is not defensible, which is why credit refusals sit at grade 1 in Table 1."
- §3.3: "Lending decisions are typically made in high volume, which makes individual review costly and turns the design question into one of what review is possible at scale."
- Table 1: "Favourable approvals, such as low-cost approvals | 2 | Audit; errors correctable; asymmetry premise checked for the domain"
- Problem: §5's "or" lets favourable outcomes reach grade 2 without review that fits the workflow; §6.4 makes that review, logging, seeded errors and trigger monitoring jointly necessary, and rules out grade 2 where high-volume review is impossible, which §3.3 calls lending's normal condition. The approvals row lists none of §6.4's conditions, and "low-cost approvals" is undefined. By §6.4's own logic an unreviewed grade-2 approval is grade 4 with a human's name attached, which §5 forbids for decisions about persons.
- Weakens: the asymmetry premise's only application, and §1's claim that AI may hold authority "in some lending and administrative settings, over favourable decisions".
- Smallest fix (Option A): §5 to *Grade 2 suits favourable or cheaply corrected outcomes where review fits the workflow.*; add *review fits the workflow; overrides logged; seeded errors* to the approvals row and define "low-cost" there. Also change §6.4: add *This applies to approvals as well as refusals.* Option B, a values choice, treats high-volume approvals as grade-3 envelopes; it would change the approvals ceiling, §5's grade-3 sentence, §6.4 and §1.

**C2. R2's statutory route covers welfare; Table 1 does not.** Conflict. **High.**
- R2: "Adverse determinations, including denials, sanctions and debts, need a named human decision-maker of record unless a statute expressly licenses grade 2 with stated review capacity and audit."
- Table 1: "Welfare denials, debt raising and sanctions | 1 | Named human decision-maker of record"
- Table 1: "Credit refusals | 1 | Named human decision-maker of record; grade 2 only under a statute that sets review capacity and audit"
- §3.3: "What is at stake is a person's income and standing, imposed by an adverse decision that no official had individually reviewed, against people least able to contest it."
- Problem: R2 extends the statutory licence to every adverse determination, welfare debts and sanctions included; Table 1 confines it to credit refusals. Under R2 a statute could license a grade-2 debt-raising system, which is the paper's own Robodebt scenario; the Commonwealth conceded in 2019 that Robodebt's income averaging was unlawful (W7).
- Weakens: the welfare ceiling and the lesson the paper draws from Robodebt.
- Smallest fix: R2's second sentence to *Adverse determinations, including denials, sanctions and debts, need a named human decision-maker of record; for credit refusals only, a statute may license grade 2 with stated review capacity and audit.* No other passage needs to change.

**C3. The protective stop does not meet the paper's definition of grade 4.** Conflict. **Medium.**
- §2: "The system acts with no timely human veto, and its limits are absent or set by the system itself."
- §2: "If the human can change only the envelope or stop the system, it is at grade 3."
- Table 1: "Automatic protective stop that moves the system to a defined safe state | 4 | Narrow and reversible; removes no entitlement; clinician can countermand at once"
- §5: "No domain licenses grade 4 for decisions about persons, with one exception."
- §5: "A protective stop may run at grade 4 if it moves a system to a defined safe state, removes no entitlement and can be countermanded by a clinician at once."
- §6.1: "Where authority is confined to validated execution within limits, it supports grade 3 or 4 use, as in dosing or protective stops."
- Table 2: "Limits are written before operation, enforced by an automatic stop, and tested in simulated and live conditions"
- Problem: grade 4 requires limits that are absent or self-set. The stop's safe state is fixed in advance by humans and a clinician can countermand it at once, which is grade 3 by the §2 test. A grade-4 system also cannot pass the bounded-delegation test that §5 requires at grades 2–4. The "one exception" is a misclassification.
- Weakens: the thesis, which appears to admit an exception it does not need (see G2).
- Smallest fix: the row's ceiling from 4 to 3; §5's two sentences to *No domain licenses grade 4 for decisions about persons. A protective stop that moves a system to a defined safe state, removes no entitlement and can be countermanded by a clinician at once is a grade-3 function.*; in §6.1, "grade 3 or 4 use" to *grade 3 use*.

**C4. The answerability test omits traceability.** Conflict. **Medium.**
- §4.1: "The paper treats traceability as the minimal test of answerability."
- §4.1: "A gap is closed by assigning named accountability before deployment, not by holding the system responsible after harm."
- Table 2: "A named institution and a named person hold design, deployment and outcome responsibility, and can correct or withdraw the system"
- §5: "Under current institutions no one can answer for a collective harm produced by a learned discretionary policy, so the answerability condition fails."
- Problem: the Table 2 test is met by naming people who can withdraw a system; it does not test traceability. A firm can name such people for a discretionary trading system, so by the test as written answerability does not fail, contrary to §5. As written, the condition almost never binds, which invites the nominal compliance described in G1.
- Weakens: the first premise of the argument (§1) and the stated reason for the market ceiling.
- Smallest fix: add to the Table 2 test *and each outcome can be traced to a named person who understood the system well enough to foresee and correct that kind of outcome*. Also change §5 to *so the traceability limb of the answerability condition fails*, together with W2's narrowing.

**C5. The asymmetry premise is "reversed", but §6.5 only removes it.** Conflict. **Medium.**
- §5: "It should be reversed for any domain in which evidence shows that wrongful approvals cause more irreversible harm, including harm to third parties, than wrongful refusals cause to the people refused."
- §6.5: "Where evidence shows that wrongful approvals cause more irreversible harm to others than wrongful refusals cause to applicants, the premise is reversed for that domain, and approvals fall to grade 1."
- Problem: a literal reversal would give automated systems more authority over refusals than over approvals, contradicting Table 1 and R2. §6.5 shows the intended result: approvals lose their advantage.
- Weakens: the rule for when the premise fails.
- Smallest fix: in §5, "It should be reversed for any domain in which" to *It lapses for any domain in which*, adding at the end *and favourable decisions there take the grade-1 default*. Also change §6.5: "the premise is reversed for that domain" to *the premise lapses for that domain*.

**C6. §6.6 calls the tests proportionate; §5 and R1 require all five.** Conflict. **Medium.**
- §6.6: "First, the conditions are minimal standards, and the tests in Section 5 are proportionate to a deployment's stakes."
- §6.6: "A low-stakes information service needs fewer of them than a system that decides who receives scarce care."
- §5: "Authority at grades 2 to 4 is licensed only when five conditions hold."
- R1: "Require every sensitive deployment to declare its grade and the ceiling for each decision class, and to show before deployment that the five conditions pass their tests (Table 2)."
- Problem: §5 has no proportionality rule. All five conditions apply at grades 2–4 and none at grades 0–1, so an information service needs none, not "fewer". Read literally, R1 makes every sensitive deployment, grades 0 and 1 included, pass all five.
- Weakens: the reply to the low-resource objection.
- Smallest fix: §6.6 to *First, the conditions apply only at grades 2 to 4, so an information service needs none of them.* Also change R1 to *and, for each decision class at grade 2 or above, to show before deployment that the five conditions pass their tests (Table 2)*. If G1's fix is adopted, add to both *and grade-1 adverse classes must pass the real-override test*.

**C7. §6.6's fallback, escalation, needs the review capacity it assumes is absent.** Conflict. **Medium.**
- §6.6: "Second, where review capacity is absent, the appropriate response is to fund that capacity or to restrict the system to escalation and information, not to accept grade 2 without review."
- Table 1: "Escalating a case for urgent clinician review | 2 | Clinician can override within the workflow; reviews sampled for seeded errors"
- Problem: escalation is a grade-2 class that needs reviewers and seeded-error sampling, so it is not available where review capacity is absent.
- Weakens: the low-resource reply.
- Smallest fix: in §6.6, "to restrict the system to escalation and information" to *to restrict the system to information (grade 0)*. No other passage. G7 takes up the larger question.

**C8. R5 widens the anti-munition class to "objects".** Conflict. **Medium.**
- R5: "keep defensive envelopes against objects bounded and stoppable"
- Table 1: "Defensive engagement of incoming munitions, not persons | 3 | Human-set envelope; human on the loop; stop available"
- Problem: "objects" include crewed aircraft, vehicles and vessels, and engaging them is force against persons, which §5 puts at grade 1.
- Weakens: the military ceiling for persons.
- Smallest fix: in R5, "against objects" to *against incoming munitions*. G5 adds the rule for tracks that may be crewed.

**C9. R6 implies that public justification licenses discretionary market AI.** Conflict. **Medium.**
- R6: "and prohibit discretionary AI market interventions without public justification"
- Table 1: "Discretionary AI market interventions | 1 | Public justification; no unilateral action"
- Problem: R6 reads as permitting such interventions once they are publicly justified; Table 1 caps them at grade 1 (advice) whatever the justification.
- Weakens: the market recommendation.
- Smallest fix: R6's clause to *and keep discretionary AI market interventions at grade 1, as advice to a publicly accountable decision-maker who gives public justification*.

**C10. Tests usable "without further philosophical work", yet thresholds and metrics are left open.** Conflict. **Medium.**
- §5: "The tests are designed so that a hospital committee, procurement office or regulator can apply them without further philosophical work."
- §5: "The thresholds are for each certifying body to set, because the evidence needed to justify numerical values does not yet exist."
- §4.3: "Fairness is a choice of target and metric, and no metric is neutral."
- Problem: setting the subgroup-error threshold and choosing which error to equalise are values choices, as §4.3 itself says. From my own knowledge, when base rates differ between groups, calibration and equal error rates cannot all hold at once (Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan, 2016; Chouldechova, 2017), so the choice cannot be avoided.
- Weakens: §1's claim that committees can apply the tables as they stand.
- Smallest fix: §5 to *can apply them; choosing thresholds and the fairness metric remains a values decision that each body must make and publish.* Also change the subgroup trigger in §5 to *a subgroup error gap, on a metric named in advance, above a threshold set in advance*.

**C11. The conclusion's "one test" drops three conditions.** Conflict. **Medium.**
- §7.2: "Any future extension of authority should pass one test."
- §7.2: "Does it preserve someone who can answer for the decision, and a route by which the person affected can be heard?"
- §6.4: "The strongest objection is that oversight becomes a formality."
- Problem: the single test covers answerability and contestability only, so a rubber-stamp deployment with a named person and an appeal route passes it.
- Weakens: the conclusion that readers are most likely to quote.
- Smallest fix: §7.2 to *Any future extension of authority should pass the five tests in Table 2. At their core is one question: does it preserve someone who can answer for the decision, and a route by which the person affected can be heard?*

**C12. The credit row exceeds its own ceiling.** Conflict. **Low.** §5: "A ceiling is the highest grade a decision class may take, given the conditions." Table 1: "Credit refusals | 1 | Named human decision-maker of record; grade 2 only under a statute that sets review capacity and audit". Weakens the ceiling definition. Fix: show the ceiling as *1 (2 under statute)* and add to §5's definition *or, where the table names a statute, the grade that statute may license*.

**C13. The Abstract calls grades 0 and 1 authority.** Conflict. **Low.** Abstract: "It distinguishes five grades of authority, from information to autonomous execution". §2: "Grades 0 and 1 are not authority". Weakens terminological consistency. Fix: Abstract to *It distinguishes five grades of system influence, from information to autonomous execution, of which the top three are authority*.

**C14. "Two dynamics", three listed.** Conflict. **Low.** §4.6: "Two dynamics are missing from a static account of grades." and "A third dynamic concerns detection." Fix: *Three dynamics are missing from a static account of grades.*

**C15. §1 promises "one case per domain".** Conflict. **Low.** §1: "Section 3 gives one case per domain". §3.3 has three: "The Australian Robodebt scheme is a documented welfare case.", "Credit scoring follows the same logic." and "Automated market interventions raise a different stake." Fix: §1 to *Section 3 gives cases from each domain*.

**C16. R4 says investigate; §5 says downgrade.** Conflict. **Low.** R4: "treat a near-zero override rate as a signal to investigate, not as proof of accuracy". §5: "an override rate that falls to near zero over a sustained period without an audit explanation". Weakens the downgrade rule. Fix: R4 to *treat a near-zero override rate as a downgrade trigger unless an audit explains it (Section 5), not as proof of accuracy*.

**C17. Two kinds of stop used interchangeably.** Conflict. **Low.** Abstract: "Bounded delegation is defensible for narrow functions whose limits humans set in advance and can enforce with a stop." Table 1: "Dosing within clinician-set limits | 3 | Limits fixed in advance; stop available". Table 2: "Limits are written before operation, enforced by an automatic stop, and tested in simulated and live conditions". An automatic stop that enforces limits and a human halt are different safeguards, and the grade-3 rows do not say which they need. Fix: define both in §2 (*automatic limit stop*, *human halt*) and write *automatic limit stop; human halt available* in Table 1's grade-3 rows.

**C18. §6.5 counts the applicant as someone other than the applicant.** Conflict. **Low.** §6.5: "A wrongful approval can harm people other than the applicant: a borrower who takes on debt they cannot carry, a lender that absorbs losses, or the public that bears the cost of a bad allocation." The borrower is the applicant, so that harm is first-party harm the premise should already weigh. Weakens the third-party reply. Fix: *A wrongful approval can harm the applicant, who takes on debt they cannot carry, and people other than the applicant: a lender that absorbs losses, or the public that bears the cost of a bad allocation.*

### 4.2 Gaps

**G1. Grade 1 is an untested safe harbour.** Gap. **High.**
- §2: "Grades 0 and 1 are not authority, but advice shapes what humans do, and the failures discussed in Section 4 arise there too."
- §2: "A human selects and remains the decision-maker of record."
- §4.5: "This affects every grade above advice, and a reviewer who never disagrees exercises no real authority."
- §5: "Authority at grades 2 to 4 is licensed only when five conditions hold."
- §6.4: "When these conditions fail, the system drops to grade 1."
- Table 1: "Welfare denials, debt raising and sanctions | 1 | Named human decision-maker of record"
- Problem: six of Table 1's twelve classes, including every adverse determination about a person and all force against persons, sit at grade 1, where no condition, test or trigger applies, and grade 1 is also the fallback when grade-2 oversight fails. Yet §2 says the Section 4 failures arise at grade 1, and §4.5 wrongly confines automation bias to grades "above advice". The sources I read point the other way. The AI Act asks that people overseeing a high-risk system stay aware of automation bias, in particular for systems that give information or recommendations to human deciders (Article 14(4)(b)). The CJEU held that a credit score a lender "draws strongly" on is itself an automated decision (SCHUFA, 7 December 2023). The CNAS account of the 2003 Patriot fratricides describes over-trust in automatic mode, with an operator's presence at the console treated as human control. A systematically biased model lowered clinicians' diagnostic accuracy by more than 11%, and image-based explanations did not mitigate the harm (Jabbour et al., JAMA, 2023, as reported by Healthcare IT News). From my own knowledge, a nominal decision-maker of record can become a *moral crumple zone* who absorbs blame for a system they could not control (Elish, 2019).
- Weakens: the central recommendation. Keeping adverse determinations at grade 1 is meant to keep authority with humans, but nothing checks that it does.
- Smallest fix: add to §2, after the grade test, *A decision class counts as grade 1 only if its human deciders pass the real-override test in Table 2; a class that fails is treated as grade 2.* Also change §4.5 "every grade above advice" to *every grade from advice upward*; §6.4 "the system drops to grade 1" to *the system drops to grade 1, and is withdrawn from the decision class if its deciders then fail the real-override test*; add *real-override test passed* to the conditions of Table 1's grade-1 adverse rows; apply §5's triggers and R4 to grade-1 adverse classes; edit R1 and §6.6 as in C6.

**G2. The term "final authority" is undefined, so the thesis appears contradicted by Table 1.** Gap. **High.**
- Abstract: "argues that AI should not hold final authority over determinations about particular persons in medicine, military force or the economy"
- §1: "AI systems should not hold final decision authority over determinations about particular persons in medicine, military force or the economy."
- §2: "A decision about a person means any determination that changes a person's treatment, legal or social status, liberty, money or access to a service."
- §2: "If the human can change only the envelope or stop the system, it is at grade 3."
- Table 1: "Dosing within clinician-set limits | 3 | Limits fixed in advance; stop available"
- Table 1: the protective-stop row quoted in C3.
- Problem: the paper never says which grades count as final authority. A dose changes a person's treatment, so dosing is a decision about a person, and at grade 3 no human can change an individual dose before it takes effect. If "final" means grade 3 or 4, Table 1 contradicts the thesis twice; if it means grade 4 only, it contradicts it once, until C3 is fixed. A reader cannot tell what the thesis forbids.
- Weakens: the central recommendation itself.
- Smallest fix: add to §2 *A system holds final authority over a decision about a person when it settles that person's outcome at grade 4.* Restate the thesis in the Abstract and §1 in two limbs: *AI systems should not hold final (grade-4) authority over any decision about a person, and adverse, value-laden and force-against-persons determinations should stay with a named human decision-maker at grade 1, subject only to the statutory exception for credit refusals.* Also change the first two sentences of §7.2 to match, and fix C3 in the same edit.

**G3. The asymmetry premise ignores scarcity.** Gap. **High.**
- Abstract: "where a wrongful adverse decision is harder to reverse than a wrongful favourable one, automated systems may be given more authority over favourable decisions than over adverse ones"
- §3.1: "Consider a commercial algorithm that health systems used to identify patients for additional care management, a scarce programme of nurse outreach and extra attention."
- §1: "Hospitals rank patients for scarce beds and specialist review."
- Table 1: "Favourable approvals, such as low-cost approvals | 2 | Audit; errors correctable; asymmetry premise checked for the domain"
- §5: "Grade 1 governs individual adverse determinations, value-laden allocation and force against persons."
- Problem: where a good is scarce, a favourable decision for one person is an adverse decision for another, whose loss is invisible and cannot be contested. The paper's own lead case shows this. In the summary I read of Obermeyer et al., patients at or above the 97th percentile of the risk score were automatically identified for enrolment, and Black patients were 17.7% of those identified, against 46.5% without the bias. The harm came from an automated favourable selection. The premise rests on an unstated assumption that favourable decisions take nothing from others.
- Weakens: the asymmetry premise (Abstract, §5), the approvals row, and the escalation carve-out (W6).
- Smallest fix: add to §5's asymmetry paragraph *The premise applies only where a favourable decision for one person does not reduce what others receive. Where the good is scarce, a favourable decision is an allocation decision and takes the allocation ceiling (grade 1).* Also change: add *non-rivalrous* to the approvals row; add to §3.1 that selection above the 97th percentile was automatic, citing the primary paper; add displaced applicants to the third-party harms in §6.5.

**G4. The paper never classifies its own military case or states the line between dosing and force.** Gap. **Medium.**
- §3.2: "A commander authorises a system to engage targets within a defined area for a fixed period, because communications are expected to be degraded and approval could not be obtained in time."
- §3.2: "The area contains combatants, civilians and people who have surrendered."
- §6.3: "It does not justify grade 4 for decisions about persons, because a bounded envelope, rather than per-action approval, is what makes speed tolerable."
- §5: "Grade 3 suits small decision spaces with limits set in advance and a reliable stop."
- §4.2: "In lethal decisions the requirement is sharpest, because the person judged cannot contest the decision at all."
- R5: "Prohibit delegated authority to select and engage persons with force."
- Problem: the §3.2 deployment is a bounded envelope, and §6.3's reasoning seems to license bounded envelopes, yet the paper never applies its own test to the case. Degraded communications remove the human halt that grade 3 requires, while human-set limits keep the case out of grade 4 as §2 defines it, so it fits no grade. Nor does the paper say why bounded delegation is acceptable for dosing, a decision about a person that can kill if it goes wrong, but not for force. §4.2 says contest is impossible in lethal decisions without saying what takes its place.
- Weakens: the military limb of the thesis and R5.
- Smallest fix: add to §5 *The Section 3.2 deployment is force against persons, whose ceiling is grade 1. It also fails grade 3 on its own terms, because degraded communications remove the human halt. Dosing differs in three ways: it is beneficent and consented, where force is adverse and unconsented; its decision space is a physical range fixed in advance, where telling combatants from civilians and from people who have surrendered needs judgement that cannot be fixed in advance; and a wrong dose can be detected and contested, where a person killed cannot contest.* Also change §6.3 "is what makes speed tolerable" to *is what makes speed tolerable for functions whose decision space can be fixed in advance*, and add to §4.2 *so the human judgement must come before the act*.

**G5. The anti-munition row has no rule for crewed tracks, and the ICRC summary omits two limits.** Gap. **Medium.**
- Table 1: "Defensive engagement of incoming munitions, not persons | 3 | Human-set envelope; human on the loop; stop available"
- §3.2: "It recommends restricting others through limits on target types, duration, geographic scope and scale, with human control and judgement retained (ICRC, 2021)."
- §3.2: "The recommendation is not binding law, and states hold differing positions."
- Problem: "not persons" describes correct functioning, not something the envelope guarantees. In 2003, Patriot batteries destroyed a British Tornado that the system classified as an anti-radiation missile and a US Navy F/A-18 engaged on a false track (CNAS). The ICRC position of 12 May 2021 also limits situations of use to those where civilians and civilian objects are absent, and requires human–machine interaction that ensures effective supervision and timely intervention and deactivation. The paper omits both limits, and omits that the ICRC recommends new legally binding rules.
- Weakens: the grade-3 defensive ceiling and the ICRC summary on which R5 relies.
- Smallest fix: the row's conditions to *Human-set envelope that excludes tracks that may be crewed, or requires a human decision for them; operators trained and tested with seeded tracks; human on the loop; stop available*. Also change §3.2 to add the two omitted limits and the ICRC's call for new binding rules.

**G6. Ceilings can fall but never rise.** Gap. **Medium.**
- §5: "The ceilings are design judgements, not empirical findings."
- §5: "A deployment is downgraded by one grade when a trigger is met, until its certifying body re-certifies it."
- §6.1: "That is an empirical question, and the framework makes it testable through subgroup audit and the override tests in Section 5."
- Problem: the paper calls the lives-saved question testable but names no result that would change a ceiling, so evidence cannot revise its restraint.
- Weakens: the reply to the lives-saved objection and the claim that authority tracks the stakes.
- Smallest fix: add to §5 *A ceiling may rise by one grade for a decision class only when prospective evidence shows outcomes no worse in every monitored subgroup, the contestability test is met at the higher grade, and the change is made by the body that sets ceilings, not by a deployer.* Also change §6.1's second reply to refer to this rule, and §8 to say that no class has yet met it.

**G7. "Adverse" has no baseline, so the low-resource objection is only half answered.** Gap. **Medium.**
- §6.6: "A strict ceiling may withhold benefit from people who would be better served by an imperfect automated service than by none."
- Table 1: "Deprioritising a patient or withholding scarce care | 1 | Clinician decides, with reasons recorded"
- Problem: WHO's 2021 TB screening guideline (Recommendation 10; conditional, low certainty of evidence) allows computer-aided detection software to be used in place of human readers of digital chest X-rays for screening and triage, among people aged 15 and over in populations where screening is recommended. A negative automated screen withholds a confirmatory test with no human decision, which Table 1 appears to put at grade 1. Whether it is adverse depends on the baseline: against human reading it may be; against no screening it is a gain. The paper does not say which baseline counts.
- Weakens: §6.6's reply and the medical ceilings.
- Smallest fix: the author chooses (D1) and states the choice in §2 and §6.6. Either *A decision is adverse if it leaves the person worse off than the service they would otherwise receive*, with local validation, audit and a sunset, or a statement that the paper accepts ruling out such screening and why. Also change §5's asymmetry paragraph to use the same definition.

**G8. Seeded errors are not specified as synthetic.** Gap. **Medium.**
- Table 2: "Reviewers have the time, information and competence to disagree, and their disagreement is measured with seeded errors"
- Table 1: "Clinician can override within the workflow; reviews sampled for seeded errors"
- R4: "seed reviews with known errors"
- Problem: inserting wrong recommendations into live clinical or benefit decisions could harm the person if the reviewer misses them, and the paper says neither whether seeded errors are live or synthetic nor who approves them. From my own knowledge, aviation-security screening projects fictitious threat images into real bag images, so screeners' vigilance is measured with no risk to anyone.
- Weakens: the real-override test, which depends on seeding.
- Smallest fix: Table 2 to *measured with seeded synthetic cases that cannot change any real person's outcome, approved by an ethics body*. Also change R4 and the escalation row's conditions to the same wording.

**G9. The deployer certifies the condition most likely to fail.** Gap. **Medium.**
- Table 2: "Real override | Reviewers have the time, information and competence to disagree, and their disagreement is measured with seeded errors | Internal audit using sampled cases"
- §6.4: "The strongest objection is that oversight becomes a formality."
- Problem: apart from answerability, every condition has an external certifier; real override, the subject of what the paper calls its strongest objection, is certified by the deployer's own internal audit.
- Weakens: the real-override condition.
- Smallest fix: the certifier to *Independent auditor or regulator, using internal audit's sampled cases*; the minimum version is internal audit with independent spot checks (D3).

**G10. The triggers miss the label loop and detection delay.** Gap. **Medium.**
- §5: "These triggers are the working form of the drift risks in Section 4.6."
- §5: "and any change to the outcome label"
- §4.6: "In welfare and fraud systems, flagged cases lead to investigations, and the findings become training labels, so the model learns its own enforcement pattern."
- §4.6: "For collective harms, such as market failures or some military effects, the delay between harm and detection is longer, which weakens the requirement that errors be correctable."
- Problem: in a label loop the label's definition does not change, only its source, so the trigger on a change to the outcome label never fires; and no trigger addresses detection delay.
- Weakens: §5's claim to have turned the drift risks into triggers, and R7.
- Smallest fix: add two triggers to §5: *training labels drawn mainly from cases the system itself flagged, without an independent random sample* and *a lag in detecting harm longer than the re-check interval*. Also change R7 to name them.

**G11. The conditions are not mapped to grades.** Gap. **Medium.**
- §5: "Authority at grades 2 to 4 is licensed only when five conditions hold."
- Table 2: "Limits are written before operation, enforced by an automatic stop, and tested in simulated and live conditions"
- Table 2: "The affected person can obtain the basis of the decision in usable terms, and a review by someone who can reverse it, within a stated time"
- Table 2: "Reviewers have the time, information and competence to disagree, and their disagreement is measured with seeded errors"
- Problem: real override and contestability are defined case by case, bounded delegation by envelope. The paper does not say what an automatic stop is for a grade-2 escalation flag, what real override means at grade 3 where no one approves each act, or who the affected person is when the target is a munition.
- Weakens: §1's promise that the tables can be applied as they stand.
- Smallest fix: add to Table 2 a note stating how each condition is met at grades 2, 3 and 4 (for example, at grade 2 the override window is the bound; at grade 3 override works on the envelope and on pattern review), dropping none.

**G12. "Discretionary AI market interventions" has no defined scope.** Gap. **Medium.**
- §5: "Discretionary interventions, in which a system chooses when and how to act on prices, do not."
- §1: "Lenders use models to refuse credit, and trading systems act on prices in milliseconds."
- R6: "Keep market automation within public, pre-committed rules with halts and kill switches, and prohibit discretionary AI market interventions without public justification."
- Problem: as defined, the class covers any AI trading system, so R6 would bar most private AI trading that lacks public justification. If the paper means interventions by public authorities, it does not say so.
- Weakens: the market ceiling and R6.
- Smallest fix: define the term in §3.3, for example *Market interventions are actions by public authorities, or market-wide controls, that set or move prices for others; private firms' trading, which existing algorithmic-trading rules govern, is out of scope*, or argue the wider claim. Also change the §5 sentence and R6 to use the defined term.

**G13. The law most directly on point is missing.** Gap. **Medium.**
- §8: "The jurisdictions discussed are few: the European Union's AI Act and the Australian Robodebt case."
- §3.3: "A refusal can exclude a person from housing, and the model's reasons are often unavailable to the applicant."
- Problem: GDPR Article 22 already gives a right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing that has legal or similarly significant effects. It allows such decisions when necessary for a contract, with explicit consent, or under a law that sets suitable safeguards, and in the contract and consent cases requires at least human intervention, a chance to give one's view and a right to contest. That is the structure of the paper's own statutory route. The SCHUFA judgment extends Article 22 to scores that decide outcomes in practice, which supports G1. The AI Act's Article 14(4) names automation bias and requires that overseers can disregard, override or reverse an output and halt the system in a safe state. In the US, creditors must give specific, accurate reasons for adverse action even when they use complex models (CFPB Circular 2022-03). Engaging these would show that the thesis tracks existing law and make clear where it goes further.
- Weakens: the paper's legal grounding and §8's account of its limits.
- Smallest fix: one paragraph in §4.2 or §6.4 citing the primary texts, and an updated §8 sentence (see V3).

**G14. The leading alternative state standard on force is not answered.** Gap. **Medium.**
- §3.2: "The recommendation is not binding law, and states hold differing positions."
- Problem: US Department of Defense Directive 3000.09 (updated January 2023) requires "appropriate levels of human judgment over the use of force", adds a senior review before development and before fielding, and does not prohibit autonomous systems that apply force to persons. The paper's prohibition needs to say why that standard is not enough.
- Weakens: R5 and the military limb against its strongest institutional rival.
- Smallest fix: a paragraph in §6.3 stating the US standard and the paper's reply, for example that "appropriate" sets no floor, and that judgement exercised before a deployment cannot recognise a person who surrenders after it.

**G15. No one is named to check the asymmetry ordering, and no standard of evidence is set.** Gap. **Medium.**
- Table 1: "Favourable approvals, such as low-cost approvals | 2 | Audit; errors correctable; asymmetry premise checked for the domain"
- R2: "Apply the asymmetry premise in law and procurement only where it has been checked for the domain."
- §5: "Where the ordering is not supported at all, the default is grade 1."
- Problem: the rule does not say who checks, on what evidence, or what happens when evidence is contested rather than absent; a deployer could check its own premise.
- Weakens: the asymmetry premise's only safeguard.
- Smallest fix: §5 to *The ordering is checked by the body that sets ceilings, on published evidence; where evidence is absent, weak or contested, the default is grade 1.*

**G16. The override-rate trigger measures agreement, not vigilance.** Gap. **Low.** §5: "an override rate that falls to near zero over a sustained period without an audit explanation". As an assumption, a rate trigger can be satisfied by token overrides, while the seeded-error catch rate already used in Table 2 is harder to game. Weakens the triggers. Fix: add the trigger *a seeded-error catch rate below a threshold set in advance*.

**G17. The downgrade rule names no re-certifier and no fallback.** Gap. **Low.** §5: "A deployment is downgraded by one grade when a trigger is met, until its certifying body re-certifies it." Table 2 lists five certifiers, and for some functions, such as closed-loop dosing, the next grade down may be unworkable. Weakens the downgrade rule. Fix: *until the body that certified the failed condition re-certifies it; where the lower grade is unworkable, the deployment is suspended for that decision class.*

**G18. The evidence is one-sided.** Gap. **Low.** §8: "It states conditions and tests, but it does not measure how often any deployment meets them, and the ceilings in Table 1 are design judgements." The restrictions rest on failure cases; nothing is cited for the grade-3 permissions (dosing, defence against munitions, market rules) or for the error rates of the human processes that automation replaced. Weakens the empirical framing of the ceilings. Fix: one citation per grade-3 row showing safe operation, or a sentence in §8 saying none is given.

**G19. "Sensitive" is undefined, and "liberty" falls outside the domains covered.** Gap. **Low.** R1: "Require every sensitive deployment to declare its grade and the ceiling for each decision class". Abstract: "AI systems are increasingly placed inside decisions about health, life and death, liberty and livelihood." Weakens the scope of R1. Fix: define "sensitive deployment" in §2 (one that makes decisions about persons, or collective decisions, in the three domains) and say in §8 that criminal justice is out of scope.

**G20. The third reply answers an efficiency argument, not a lives argument.** Gap. **Low.** §6.1: "If efficiency justified removing review, no appeal would survive." The objection is about lives, not efficiency. Weakens the reply to the lives-saved objection. Fix: *Third, a gain in lives on average does not answer the person wrongly deprioritised, who needs a route to be heard; augmentation keeps that route.*

**G21. "Authority" is defined to include answerability.** Gap. **Low.** §2: "To say that a system has decision authority is to say that its output settles a matter others must accept, and that someone can be held to account for it." §1: "Authority requires an answerable agent and a route of challenge, which an AI system cannot supply alone." If authority includes answerability by definition, the claim that AI cannot hold authority alone is true by definition, while the real question is about decision power. Weakens the first premise against a definitional objection. Fix: add to §2 *The question is whether a system's output may settle outcomes, which this paper calls decision power; authority is decision power that someone can answer for.*

**W2. The claim that "no one can answer" for market AI.** Overstated claim. **Medium.**
- §5: "Under current institutions no one can answer for a collective harm produced by a learned discretionary policy, so the answerability condition fails."
- §3.3: "The harm is collective and fast, and no single person can answer for it."
- Found: under MiFID II Article 17, the regulatory technical standard RTS 6 requires firms that trade algorithmically to have clear lines of accountability (Article 1), a person designated to authorise deployment (Article 5(2)), a kill function that cancels unexecuted orders (Article 12), and pre-trade controls with automated throttles (Article 15).
- Difference: firms answer for their own algorithms. What has no answerable party is harm that emerges from the interaction of many firms' algorithms.
- Weakens: the stated reason for the grade-1 market ceiling.
- Smallest fix: §5 to *Existing algorithmic-trading rules make each firm answer for its own algorithms, but no one answers for harm that emerges from many learned policies acting together, so the answerability condition fails for discretionary interventions that act on market-wide prices.* Also change §3.3's sentence to match.

**W3. The Obermeyer study is cited for something it did not show.** Overstated attribution. **Medium.**
- §6.1: "A system that saves more lives overall can still under-serve a subgroup, as the Obermeyer case shows (Obermeyer et al., 2019)."
- Found: the study measured bias from a cost label in patient selection; it did not measure lives saved.
- Weakens: the first reply to the lives-saved objection and the paper's credibility with careful readers.
- Smallest fix: *A system that saves more lives overall can still under-serve a subgroup; the Obermeyer case shows how a well-calibrated label can do so (Obermeyer et al., 2019).*

**W4. Human bias "heterogeneous", automated bias "uniform".** Overstated claim. **Medium.**
- §6.2: "Human bias is heterogeneous."
- §6.2: "Automated bias is uniform and scaled."
- §6.2: "One flawed label distorts every decision the system makes"
- Own knowledge (not checked here): shared, systematic human biases are documented, for example racial bias in clinicians' assessment of pain (Hoffman and colleagues, PNAS, 2016). A flawed label biases a system's decisions systematically, but it does not change every individual decision.
- Difference: the reply's real force lies in the routes for correction (officials can be questioned, retrained and replaced), not in heterogeneity.
- Weakens: the reply to "humans are biased too".
- Smallest fix: *Human bias is often, though not always, heterogeneous.* and *One flawed label biases the system's decisions in every case it handles*.

**W5. Explanation as a precondition of meaningful override.** Overstated claim. **Medium.**
- §4.4: "Without reasons, an override rests on intuition, which is a weak safeguard."
- §4.4: "Explanation is therefore a precondition of contestation and of meaningful override."
- Found: in a randomised vignette study of hospital clinicians, systematically biased model predictions lowered diagnostic accuracy by more than 11%, and commonly used image-based explanations did not mitigate the harm (Jabbour et al., JAMA, 2023, as reported by Healthcare IT News; the primary article could not be opened). Own knowledge: a professional's override can rest on an independent assessment of the case, not only on the model's reasons.
- Difference: explanation is plausibly necessary for the affected person to contest a decision; for professional override it has been shown neither necessary nor sufficient.
- Weakens: the safeguards the paper builds on explanation.
- Smallest fix: *Explanation is a precondition of contestation by the person affected. For professional override it is not enough, so the real-override test measures override directly.*

**W6. Escalation "costs little".** Overstated claim. **Medium.**
- §5: "A flag that moves a case up a queue costs little if it is wrong, and a human's judgement still settles the outcome."
- §2: "A system may therefore be trusted to escalate a case at grade 2 while being refused any power to deny one."
- §1: "Public agencies score benefit claims and flag families for fraud review."
- §4.6: the label-loop sentence quoted in G10.
- Table 1: "Escalating a case for urgent clinician review | 2 | Clinician can override within the workflow; reviews sampled for seeded errors"
- Problem: two kinds of escalation cost more. Escalation to investigation (fraud, child protection) imposes burden and stigma and feeds the label loop. In a fixed-capacity queue, moving one case up moves others down, which is the deprioritisation Table 1 puts at grade 1, and no human reviews the people displaced. From my own knowledge, excess alerts also cause alert fatigue.
- Weakens: the escalation carve-out and the grade-2 escalation row.
- Smallest fix: §5 to *A protective flag that moves a case up for review costs little if it is wrong, provided it does not displace others from a fixed-capacity queue; a flag that opens an investigation is adverse and takes grade 1.* Also change the escalation row's conditions (add *displacement of unflagged cases monitored*) and add a §5 trigger, *time to review for unflagged cases rises*.

**W7. Robodebt: "no official had individually reviewed".** Unverified at its stated strength. **Low.** §3.3: "What is at stake is a person's income and standing, imposed by an adverse decision that no official had individually reviewed, against people least able to contest it." The secondary source I read describes much reduced human oversight, not none in every case; the Royal Commission's report would settle it. The paper also omits that income averaging was conceded unlawful in 2019, which bears on C2. Weakens the Robodebt case only slightly. Fix: if the report does not support the absolute, *that, in most cases, no official had reviewed*; add the 2019 concession.

**W8. AI Act timing is not stated.** Unverified as to timing. **Low.** §6.4: "The EU's AI Act requires that high-risk systems be designed so that natural persons can effectively oversee them (European Union, 2024), but a legal requirement does not make oversight real." Article 14(1) matches the official text. A provisional agreement of 7 May 2026, which Parliament approved on 16 June 2026, would move the high-risk duties for Annex III systems to 2 December 2027 and for Annex I systems to 2 August 2028; its status at publication should be checked. Own knowledge, not checked here: the Act excludes military and defence uses. Fix: give the application date, and note the military exclusion in §8.

**W9. AI systems "cannot carry answerability or hear and reverse a challenge".** Overstated claim. **Low.** §7.2: "AI systems can carry work that humans set for them, but they cannot carry answerability or hear and reverse a challenge." From my own knowledge, automated reconsideration can re-process a challenge and reverse an output; the paper's point is normative. Fix: *they cannot answer for a decision, and hearing a challenge needs someone who can.*

### 5.1 Analytical

**Reasoning chain**
1. *Evidence.* The central recommendation has four load-bearing parts: no final AI authority over determinations about persons (Abstract, §1); bounded delegation for narrow functions (§1); the asymmetry premise (§5); and five licensing conditions with ceilings and downgrade triggers (§5, Tables 1–2).
2. *Evidence.* Table 1 lists 12 decision classes: six at grade 1, two at grade 2, three at grade 3 and one at grade 4. The five conditions apply only at grades 2–4 (§5).
3. *Inference.* The tests therefore reach half the classes, and the half they miss holds every adverse determination about a person and all force against persons (G1).
4. *Inference.* Under the paper's own definitions, two rows let a system settle a person's treatment with no per-case human control (dosing at grade 3, the protective stop at grade 4), yet "final authority" is never mapped to grades (G2). The stop fails the paper's definition of grade 4 (C3), and the §3.2 military case, having lost its human halt, fits no grade and is never classified (G4).
5. *Inference.* The asymmetry premise, said to govern direction, is used in one row, and that row conflicts with §6.4 (C1); its failure rule says "reversed" where it means *lapses* (C5). It is worth keeping, narrowed to the cases where it holds.
6. *Inference.* Checking each rule against its restatements turns up many further conflicts (§4.1), nearly all fixable with a sentence. The direction is coherent; the operational layer is not yet consistent, so the definitions and tables should be repaired before anything is added.

**Key concepts**
- Definition of "final authority": 0.9. Without it the thesis cannot be checked against Table 1.
- Test coverage by grade: 0.85. The tests miss the most sensitive half of the classes.
- Consistency of tables and text: 0.75. Committees will apply the tables, not the prose.
- Reach of the asymmetry premise: 0.5. Billed as governing, it is applied once.

### 5.2 Creative

**Reasoning chain**
1. *Evidence.* The paper treats a grade as something a deployment declares, and "favourable" or "adverse" as a property of a decision's content (§2, §5).
2. *Own knowledge.* Aviation-security screening projects fictitious threat images into real bag images, so screeners' detection is measured on live work with no risk to anyone.
3. *Inference.* Reframe 1, the measured grade: a class is at the grade its humans' behaviour shows, so a nominal grade-1 class whose deciders miss seeded synthetic cases is really at grade 2 (G1, G8).
4. *Inference.* Reframe 2, eligibility against allocation: the asymmetry premise fits decisions that take nothing from others; where goods are scarce (beds, care-management places), a favourable decision for one person is an adverse decision for another (G3, W6).
5. *Inference.* Reframe 3, the baseline: count a decision as adverse only against what the person would otherwise receive, which turns §6.6's worry into a rule (G7). Reframe 4, two-way ceilings: staged licensing with criteria for rising as well as triggers for falling (G6); and for force, a line between targets fixed by physical signature, such as an incoming munition, and targets defined by status or conduct, such as combatant, civilian or surrendering, which need judgement no envelope can fix in advance (G4).
6. *Inference.* These reframings let the paper keep its thesis while answering its hardest objections; each carries values the author must own.

**Key concepts**
- Measured grade: 0.85. It closes the gap between declared and real human control.
- Eligibility against allocation: 0.8. It puts the asymmetry premise where it holds.
- Baseline-relative "adverse": 0.6. It answers the low-resource objection but invites a two-tier standard (D1).
- Two-way ceilings: 0.6. They let evidence revise the restraint.
- Signature against status: 0.55. It gives the military line a principle, though misclassification remains (D5).

### 5.3 Critical

**Reasoning chain**
1. *Evidence.* The paper's safeguard for every adverse determination is a named human decision-maker at grade 1, and its remedy when grade-2 oversight fails is to drop to grade 1 (Table 1, §6.4).
2. *Evidence.* The sources I read place automation bias exactly there. The AI Act asks that overseers stay aware of it, in particular for systems that give recommendations to human deciders (Article 14(4)(b)); the CJEU treats a score that a lender draws strongly on as an automated decision (SCHUFA); CNAS describes Patriot crews in 2003 over-trusting automatic mode, and two crewed aircraft destroyed by a system meant for missiles; biased model predictions lowered clinicians' diagnostic accuracy and explanations did not help (Jabbour et al., 2023, news report).
3. *Own knowledge.* A nominal decision-maker of record can become a *moral crumple zone* who absorbs blame for a system they could not control (Elish, 2019). A table that asks only for a named human invites exactly that.
4. *Inference.* The strongest counterargument: if a validated system would beat human decisions for every subgroup, a fixed grade-1 ceiling costs identifiable patients and claimants, and the paper names no result that could lift a ceiling (G6). WHO already allows software in place of human readers for TB screening (G7).
5. *Inference.* The leading view fails when grade-1 review is nominal (G1), when goods are rivalrous (G3) or when the baseline is no service (G7). The asymmetry premise, an untested values choice applied in one row, is too weak to be the premise that "governs direction", and should be a conditional rule. Several replies rest on overstated claims (W1–W5).
6. *Inference.* Who bears the cost: permissiveness falls on people wrongly refused, flagged or left out, often those least able to contest; restraint falls on people who wait or go without. The paper names the second (§6.6) but leaves the first concentrated at grade 1. A baseline that treats no service as the comparison risks a two-tier standard in which poorer populations get machine decisions.

**Key concepts**
- Nominal human control: 0.9. It is the central safeguard's weakest point.
- Harm to the people left out under scarcity: 0.7. The paper's own case shows it.
- Who bears the cost: 0.6. Legitimacy depends on where errors fall.
- Restraint with no route upward: 0.6. The position cannot learn from evidence.
- Overstated supporting claims: 0.45. They weaken the replies, not the thesis.

### 5.4 Systems thinking

**Reasoning chain**
1. *Evidence.* The paper names authority creep and the label loop, notes detection delay (§4.6), and calls its triggers their working form (§5).
2. *Inference.* Both named dynamics are reinforcing loops: shorter windows bring fewer overrides, less practice and more deference; flags bring investigations, labels and more flags of the same kind.
3. *Assumption.* Two further loops are likely: excess escalations breed alert fatigue (a known clinical pattern, from my own knowledge), and a trigger on override rates invites token overrides.
4. *Inference.* The triggers catch creep but not the label loop, whose label keeps its definition while its source changes, nor detection delay, and they measure agreement rather than vigilance (G10, G16). In fixed-capacity queues, escalating or selecting one person displaces others whose harm no one reviews (W6, G3).
5. *Inference.* Incentives: the deployer's internal audit certifies the condition most likely to fail (G9); a statutory route to grade 2 invites sector-by-sector statutes (C2, C12; the ratchet is an assumption); competitive pressure is rightly treated as a coordination problem (§6.3).
6. *Inference.* Over years, deskilling erodes even a real grade-1 review, so grade-1 safeguards need monitoring over time, not one-off certification (G1).

**Key concepts**
- Unmonitored reinforcing loops: 0.75. The label loop and detection delay have no trigger.
- Displacement in scarce queues: 0.7. Harm lands on people no one reviews.
- Certifier incentives: 0.6. The deployer certifies its own weakest point.
- Deskilling over time: 0.6. Safeguards decay unless monitored.
- Statutory ratchet: 0.35. Plausible, but without evidence.

### 5.5 Pragmatic

**Reasoning chain**
1. *Evidence.* The paper's readers, namely committees, procurement officers, regulators and ethics boards, are meant to apply Tables 1–2 directly (§1, §5).
2. *Inference.* Four things block that use as written: open thresholds and metrics despite the claim that no philosophical work remains (C10); conditions not mapped to grades (G11); no named re-certifier or fallback (G17); and seeded errors not specified as synthetic (G8).
3. *Evidence.* Some certifying machinery already exists: EU algorithmic-trading rules require accountable governance, a designated approver, a kill function and pre-trade controls (RTS 6), and the AI Act expects a stop that brings a system to a safe state (Article 14(4)(e)). From my own knowledge, states party to Additional Protocol I must review new weapons (Article 36).
4. *Assumption.* Independent algorithm auditors and welfare-oversight capacity are thinner than the tables assume, and seeded review, subgroup reporting and audit cost staff and data, as R8 itself says.
5. *Inference.* The definitional and table repairs are cheap, about a sentence each, and remove most conflicts; the grade-1 test and the rivalry clause cost more; piloting the tables on two or three real deployments costs most and would persuade most.
6. *Inference.* Editing the paper is reversible, but a regulator adopting an inconsistent table is hard to undo: repair before circulating, then re-audit.

**Key concepts**
- Usability for certifiers: 0.8. It is the paper's stated purpose.
- Cheap consistency repairs: 0.75. They give the most gain per edit.
- A field test of the tables: 0.65. It is the main missing evidence.
- Safe design of seeded errors: 0.6. The real-override test depends on it.
- Certification capacity: 0.5. It varies by domain and country.
