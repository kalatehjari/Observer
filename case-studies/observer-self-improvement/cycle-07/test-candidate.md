# Observer analysis: how to revise "Graded Authority for AI over Sensitive Human Matters"

This is an Observer multi-lens analysis (skill version cycle-07), run at full depth for a high-stakes question on 23 October 2026. The paper is cited by section, table and recommendation number. Quotation marks enclose only text copied exactly from the paper. Suggested replacement wording is in italics.

## Summary

**The question.** What revisions would make this paper the strongest defensible case on whether AI systems should hold decision authority in medicine, military force and economic allocation? How far can its central recommendation be trusted?

**Recommendation.** Keep the paper's position and its structure: the grades, permissions assessed one at a time, the conditions, the ceilings, the downgrade triggers, and its candour about which choices are design judgements or values choices. Then repair it in this order:

1. Restate the thesis in grade terms.
2. State the missing argument for keeping adverse decisions at grade 1.
3. Make grade 1 review real.
4. Add a rule for scarce goods.
5. Make Recommendation 2 agree with Table 1.
6. Tighten the asymmetry premise, the conditions and the market section.
7. Answer the missing objections and correct the factual claims.
8. Audit the revised paper again.

**Confidence.**

- **The revision plan: 80/100.** This is the probability that the plan survives the checks still needed.
  - By part: textual repairs 90, argument repairs 75, new objection replies and narrowing of the market section 70, factual updates 85.
  - The plan is fully reversible.
- **The paper's central recommendation, scored separately: 62/100.** By part:
  - No grade 4 authority over any determination about a person: 90.
  - No delegated force against persons: 75. This is a values position that some major states resist.
  - Adverse individual determinations kept at grade 1: 60.
  - Bounded grade 2–3 delegation under the five conditions, with the listed ceilings: 50.
  - Grade 2 for favourable approvals under the asymmetry premise: 40.
  - The market recommendations as written: 35.
- **Reversibility.** If the restrictive ceilings are wrong, they can be raised later. Errors in the permissive rows (wrongful debts, denials, deaths) often cannot be undone.

**Main divergence.** For adverse decisions, which protects people better: a human decision at grade 1, or honest automation at grade 2 with a properly resourced appeal (the route GDPR Article 22 allows)?
- Part of this is factual and unmeasured: how well does each route catch errors?
- Part is a values question: is a person facing an adverse decision owed a human's consideration before it takes effect?
- The paper chooses grade 1 without giving that argument. Its own sources doubt that grade 1 review works in practice.

**Most serious problems, by severity.**

1. **A1 (high).** The thesis rests on "final authority", which is never defined, while Table 1 lets grades 2 to 4 settle determinations about persons. (Abstract; Section 1)
2. **A2 (high).** The grade 1 ceiling for adverse decisions rests on a premise the paper never states. The "one test" in Section 7.2 would pass a grade 2 welfare-denial system. (Sections 1, 5, 7.2)
3. **A3 (high).** Grade 1, the paper's main safeguard, has no tests. Yet the review the paper cites finds automation bias with advisory tools. (Sections 4.5, 5)
4. **A4 (high).** When goods are scarce, escalating or favouring one person deprioritises another. The paper's lines between escalation and deprioritisation, and between favourable and adverse decisions, fail even on its own lead case. (Section 5; Table 1 medicine rows)
5. **A5 (high).** Recommendation 2 lets a statute license grade 2 welfare debts and sanctions, which Table 1 does not allow. (Recommendation 2; Table 1)
6. **A6 (medium).** Answerability is tested by naming a person, not by the traceability the paper calls the minimum. (Section 4.1; Table 2)
7. **A8 (medium).** The paper's own military case fits no grade, and the bounded-delegation test lacks the criterion that would decide it. (Section 3.2; Table 2)
8. **A9 (medium).** The asymmetry premise has two incompatible defaults, and "reversed" names the wrong consequence. (Sections 5, 6.5)
9. **A11 (medium).** Fraud and compliance flags have no ceiling, and the paper says a wrong flag costs little. (Section 5; Table 1)
10. **A14 (medium).** The market section rests on an undefined term, falls outside the stated scope, and overstates the claim that no one can answer for market harms. (Section 5; Recommendation 6)

The audit records 12 further medium findings and 13 low ones.

**Not professional advice.** This analyses an argument. It is not legal, medical, military or financial advice. Before anyone relies on the tiers, the revised paper should be reviewed by specialists in international humanitarian law, clinical safety, administrative and welfare law, financial regulation and human factors.

## 1. Recommendation

Keep the paper's position. Revise it in this order.

1. **Restate the thesis in grades.**
   - No grade 4 over any determination about a person.
   - Nothing above grade 1 for adverse or value-laden determinations, or for force against persons.
   - Grades 2–3 only for escalation that adds review, protective stops, narrow execution, and favourable decisions that pass the asymmetry check.
   - Move the protective stop to grade 3. (A1, A7)
2. **State why adverse decisions stay at grade 1.**
   - Two empirical reasons: harm is done before any appeal can succeed, and the burden of contesting falls on the person affected.
   - One values reason: a person is owed human consideration before the decision takes effect.
   - Answer the objection that automation with an appeal would do as well.
   - Complete the test in Section 7.2. (A2)
3. **Make grade 1 real.**
   - Correct the scope of Section 4.5.
   - Apply the right-target and real-override tests to grade 1 wherever the ceiling is 1.
   - Treat a failed seeded-error test as forbidden grade 2 power. (A3)
4. **Add a scarcity rule.**
   - Escalation that displaces others counts as allocation and takes the deprioritisation ceiling; so do favourable decisions over rationed goods.
   - Decide how validated screening triage is treated, and say why. (A4)
5. **Limit Recommendation 2's statutory exception to credit refusals**, so it matches Table 1. (A5)
6. **Give the asymmetry premise one rule.**
   - It applies only after a check.
   - When the check fails, it is withdrawn, not reversed.
   - It uses one harm measure.
   - Its check gets a row in Table 2. (A9, A10, A23)
7. **Tighten the conditions.**
   - Answerability must test understanding. (A6)
   - Bounded delegation must fix the target type, and the case in Section 3.2 needs a verdict. (A8)
   - Add a row for fraud flags. (A11)
   - Map each condition to the grades it covers. (A12, A13)
   - Complete the downgrade rule, and add a fallback for when no certifier exists. (A15, A35)
8. **Narrow the market section** to interventions by public authorities or market operators. Replace "no one can answer" with the narrower point that such harm is hard to attribute. (A14)
9. **Answer the remaining strong objections.**
   - Command responsibility, and claims that machines comply with the law better. (A17)
   - AI systems that give reasons. (A18)
   - Algorithmic bias that can be found and corrected. (A20)
   - Say which ceilings evidence could raise. (A16)
10. **Correct and anchor the factual claims.**
    - Define the paper's scope. (A19)
    - Qualify the claim about credit reasons, and cite GDPR Article 22 and AI Act Articles 14(4)(b), 86 and 2(3). (A21)
    - Soften the claim that no further philosophical work is needed. (A22)
    - Fix A24–A34.
    - Add the Dutch childcare-benefits case and WHO's screening guideline.
11. **Get specialist review, then re-audit the whole revision.** Steps 1–7 change the Abstract and Sections 1, 2, 5, 6, 7 and 8 together, and edits like these often create new conflicts.

### What would change this recommendation

- If the author holds that a statute may license grade 2 adverse decisions in welfare, step 5 reverses. Table 1 would change instead, and the paper would have to argue why the Robodebt lesson does not bar it.
- If the author grounds every grade 1 ceiling on dignity alone, step 9's evidence gate drops out, along with the second reply in Section 6.1.
- If the paper is meant as a short position piece rather than operational guidance, keep steps 1–6 and 11 and drop the certification detail.
- If the CCW Review Conference in November 2026 adopts a negotiating mandate, Section 3.2 and Recommendation 5 need updating.
- If seeded-error tests prove unable to detect deference, step 3 should require a design rather than a test. One such design is to have the reviewer record a judgement before seeing the recommendation.

## 2. Convergence zones and productive divergences

All five lenses come from one model, so their agreement is not independent verification. A zone carries more weight when it rests on the paper's own text and on sources I read than when it rests on shared assumptions.

### Convergence zones

1. **Grade 1 must be made real (A3).**

The evidence is the Parasuraman and Manzey abstract, AI Act Article 14(4)(b), the Dutch case, and the Obermeyer referral channel. The assumption is that measuring disagreement, or having the reviewer judge first, can make oversight real.

The evidence is Robodebt's reversed onus and long remedy timeline, and GDPR Article 22 as the alternative model. ### 3.2 Findings

**A1 · High · Gap (key term undefined) that produces a conflict: the thesis's key term is undefined**

Where:
- Abstract: "argues that AI should not hold final authority over determinations about particular persons in medicine, military force or the economy"
- Section 1: "AI systems should not hold final decision authority over determinations about particular persons in medicine, military force or the economy."
- Section 2: "To say that a system has decision authority is to say that its output settles a matter others must accept, and that someone can be held to account for it."
- Section 2: "A decision about a person means any determination that changes a person's treatment, legal or social status, liberty, money or access to a service."
- Table 1: dosing (ceiling 3), the protective stop (4) and favourable approvals (2) are all determinations about persons as Section 2 defines them.
- Section 5: "No domain licenses grade 4 for decisions about persons, with one exception."

Problem:
- The paper never defines what makes authority "final". If it means grade 4, the thesis says less than the paper argues, because the paper also caps adverse decisions at grade 1. The protective-stop row still contradicts it.
- If it means grades 2 to 4, the dosing, stop and approval rows contradict it.
- Section 2 also puts two things in one term: an output that settles a matter, and accountability for that output. The grades measure only the first, so the thesis can read as true by definition.

Weakens: the central claim. A reader cannot tell exactly what is recommended.

Smallest fix: restate the thesis by grade. Suggested wording:
> *AI systems should hold no grade 4 authority over any determination about a particular person, and no authority above grade 1 over adverse or value-laden determinations about persons or over force against persons. They may hold grade 2 or 3 authority over escalation that adds review, protective stops, narrow execution within limits humans set and, where the asymmetry check passes, favourable decisions, if the conditions for that grade hold.*

In Section 2, add one sentence that separates *decision power* (what grades 2 to 4 measure) from *authority* (decision power held by an answerable person or institution).

Other passages the fix must change:
- The Abstract sentence.
- The thesis sentence in Section 1.
- Section 7.2: "which may delegate bounded functions to machines on conditions" becomes *which may give machines decision power at the grades Table 1 sets, on the conditions for each grade*.
- The grade 4 sentence in Section 5 (see A7).

**A2 · High · Gap (conclusion rests on an unstated premise) and conflict: why adverse decisions stay at grade 1**

Where:
- Section 1: "Authority requires an answerable agent and a route of challenge, which an AI system cannot supply alone."
- Section 4.1: "A gap is closed by assigning named accountability before deployment, not by holding the system responsible after harm."
- Section 4.2: "A person subject to a consequential decision is entitled to know its basis and to have it reviewed by someone who can reverse it, whether or not the decision is accurate."
- Section 5: "Grade 1 governs individual adverse determinations, value-laden allocation and force against persons."
- Section 6.4: "In high-volume settings where meaningful review is impossible, grade 2 is not defensible, which is why credit refusals sit at grade 1 in Table 1."
- Section 7.2: "Does it preserve someone who can answer for the decision, and a route by which the person affected can be heard?"

Problem:
- The argument shows two things: some human or institution must answer, and a human review must exist. A grade 2 system can supply both. A named institution answers, and a human hears the appeal afterwards.
- The paper itself relies on institutional answerability for grade 3 dosing and market rules. It also allows grade 2 credit refusals under statute.
- EU law already takes this route for some decisions. GDPR Article 22 bars solely automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects unless an exception applies: contract, authorisation by law, or explicit consent. Safeguards include human intervention and, in some cases, the right to contest. (Checked via a law-firm summary of the SCHUFA judgment, C-634/21.)
- So the grade 1 ceiling needs further premises, which the paper never assembles.
- Section 7.2's single test omits them. It would pass a grade 2 welfare-denial system with a named official and an appeal, which Table 1 forbids.
- Section 6.4 gives yet another reason (volume).

Weakens: the paper's main contested conclusion. The strongest objection to it is left unanswered.

Smallest fix: add a paragraph to Section 5, after the asymmetry paragraph. Suggested wording:
> *An institution can answer for a grade 2 system and a person can be given an appeal, so the grade 1 ceiling for adverse determinations rests on three further premises. First (empirical), a wrongful adverse decision does harm before any appeal succeeds, such as lost income or care, that a later reversal does not undo. Second (empirical), the burden of contesting falls on the person affected, and those most exposed to adverse decisions are often least able to bear it, so appeals find fewer errors than occur; Robodebt reversed the onus onto recipients and took years to unwind. Third (values), a person facing an adverse determination is owed consideration of their situation, before it takes effect, by someone who can answer for it. Where the first two premises fail for a decision class, the ceiling rests on the third alone, and the paper says so.*

Then add an objection reply in Section 6 on automation with an appeal, answered from these premises and from A3. In Section 7.2, add to the test: *and, for an adverse determination, a human who considers the case before it takes effect*.

Other passages the fix must change:
- Section 6.4's last sentence: make volume the reason grade 2 review would be nominal, and refer to the new paragraph.
- Section 8: list the first two premises as empirical conditions that this paper does not establish.
- The Table 1 credit row is now explained, because the premises are weaker for credit than for welfare.

**A3 · High · Conflict and gap: grade 1 is left unprotected**

Where:
- Section 2: "Grades 0 and 1 are not authority, but advice shapes what humans do, and the failures discussed in Section 4 arise there too."
- Section 4.5: "This affects every grade above advice, and a reviewer who never disagrees exercises no real authority."
- Section 5: "Authority at grades 2 to 4 is licensed only when five conditions hold."
- Section 6.4: "The strongest objection is that oversight becomes a formality."
- Table 1: "Recommending the engagement of persons" (ceiling 1), with "Human decision for each engagement"
- Recommendation 4: "Make override real."

Problem:
- Section 4.5 limits oversight failure to grades above advice. That contradicts Section 2. It also contradicts the paper's own source. Parasuraman and Manzey's abstract reports three things about automation bias. It produces errors of omission and commission when decision aids are imperfect. It occurs in experts. Training or instructions do not prevent it. (TRID record)
- The AI Act requires that overseers be kept aware of over-reliance on outputs, in particular for systems that give information or recommendations to human decision-makers (Article 14(4)(b)).
- The paper's own cases show harm passing through grade 1 channels.
  - The Obermeyer tool referred patients above the 55th percentile to their physicians (course slides summarising the study).
  - Dutch fraud flags went to civil servants for manual review before families' benefits were suspended (Amnesty, 2021).
- Yet Table 2 applies only to grades 2 to 4. So every grade 1 ceiling can be met on paper while the system makes the decision in fact. Those ceilings are the paper's main protection for welfare, credit, scarce care and targeting.
- The paper calls this the strongest objection (Section 6.4), but answers it only for grade 2.
- The systems lens adds a mechanism. When approvals are automated and refusals go to humans, the human queue holds only proposed refusals. That is where deference does most harm.

Weakens: the claim that the ceilings keep authority with humans.

Smallest fix:
- In Section 4.5, replace "every grade above advice" with *every grade, advice included*.
- In Section 5, add this suggested wording:
  > *In a decision class whose ceiling is 1, a grade 1 deployment must also pass the right-target and real-override tests. If seeded-error tests show that reviewers do not catch the system's errors, the deployment is exercising grade 2 power that the ceiling forbids, and it must be redesigned or withdrawn.*
- Optionally, name one design to test: the reviewer records a judgement before seeing the recommendation.

Other passages the fix must change:
- Recommendations 1 and 4: extend them to grade 1 deployments in ceiling-1 classes.
- Table 2: the new applicability column (see A12).
- Section 6.6: state that the added tests do not reach information services.
- Section 8: list these tests as untested.

**A4 · High · Conflict and gap: scarcity collapses the paper's lines**

Where:
- Section 3.1: "a scarce programme of nurse outreach and extra attention"
- Section 2: "Escalation that changes only a queue position is not such a determination, but it can shape one, which is why Section 5 treats it separately."
- Section 5: "A flag that moves a case up a queue costs little if it is wrong, and a human's judgement still settles the outcome."
- Table 1: "Escalating a case for urgent clinician review" (ceiling 2), against "Deprioritising a patient or withholding scarce care" (ceiling 1)
- Abstract and Section 5, the asymmetry premise: "automated systems may be given more authority over favourable decisions than over adverse ones"
- Section 6.6: "restrict the system to escalation and information"

Problem:
- When review capacity is fixed or a good is rationed, moving one case up moves others down, and a favourable decision for one person is an adverse decision for another. Escalation at grade 2 and deprioritisation at grade 1 then describe the same act from two sides.
- The asymmetry premise therefore favours exactly the automation that causes allocation harm.
- The paper's own lead case shows this. Course slides summarising the study say the Obermeyer tool automatically enrolled patients above the 97th percentile. That was a favourable, escalating decision, and the harm fell on the patients it left out.
- The paper also has no category for screening against a threshold, where a negative screen is a decision not to escalate.
- Read literally, Table 1 would cap at grade 1 a practice that WHO endorses for exactly the low-resource settings of Section 6.6. In its 2021 guideline, WHO conditionally recommends, on low-certainty evidence, that computer-aided detection may replace human readers of chest X-rays when screening and triaging people aged 15 and over for TB.

Weakens: the medical ceilings, the asymmetry premise as applied to rationed goods, and the reply in Section 6.6.

Smallest fix:
- In Section 5's escalation passage, add this suggested wording:
  > *Escalation is treated separately only when it adds review without displacing another case. Where capacity is fixed or the good is rationed, raising one case lowers others, so such escalation, and favourable decisions over rationed goods, are allocation and take the deprioritisation ceiling.*
- Add a Table 1 row for population screening triage with a validated threshold. The author sets its ceiling and states why. One option is 3, with programme-level answerability, thresholds validated for each subgroup, and a route to retesting. The other is 1, with a statement of why the paper departs from the WHO guideline.

Other passages the fix must change:
- The conditions in the Table 1 escalation row: add *adds review without displacing other cases*.
- The asymmetry paragraph: add *The premise does not apply to rationed goods.*
- Section 6.6: name screening as the main low-resource case.

**A5 · High · Conflict: the statutory exception covers welfare in Recommendation 2 but not in Table 1**

Where:
- Table 1, welfare row: "Welfare denials, debt raising and sanctions", with "Named human decision-maker of record"
- Table 1, credit row: "Named human decision-maker of record; grade 2 only under a statute that sets review capacity and audit"
- Recommendation 2: "Adverse determinations, including denials, sanctions and debts, need a named human decision-maker of record unless a statute expressly licenses grade 2 with stated review capacity and audit."

Problem: Recommendation 2 would let a statute license automated debt-raising at grade 2. That is the pattern of the paper's own Robodebt case. Table 1 allows the statutory route only for credit refusals.

## 4. Lens summaries

Each lens is labelled step by step as evidence (from the paper or a source I read), own knowledge, assumption or inference. The mind maps are plain indented outlines, because colour is not available in this Markdown file.

### Analytical

**Reasoning chain**
1. [evidence] The thesis is stated in terms of "final authority" (Abstract, Section 1). Under Section 2's definition of a decision about a person, three of Table 1's 12 rows permit grade 2 or above for such decisions: dosing (3), the protective stop (4) and favourable approvals (2). A fourth row, escalation (2), is excluded only by the escalation carve-out. The thesis term does not settle which reading is meant (A1).
2. [inference] Step 1 of the paper's argument shows that an AI system cannot supply answerability or a route of challenge on its own. That supports a narrower conclusion: an institution must answer, and a human review must exist. It does not yield grade 1 for adverse decisions, because elsewhere the paper lets institutions answer for grade 2 and grade 3 systems. A further premise is needed (A2).
3. [evidence] The grade definitions misclassify the protective stop (A7) and leave the paper's own military case without a grade (A8). Section 4.5 confines automation bias to grades above advice, which contradicts Section 2 (A3).
4. [evidence] The asymmetry premise is stated with two defaults and three harm measures (A9, A23), and it is the only licensing step with no Table 2 test (A10). Under scarcity, the line between favourable and adverse decisions that it relies on fails (A4).
5. [inference] Two documented cases and one illustrative scenario support the failure modes. By the paper's own account, no empirical work supports the specific ceilings, the drift dynamics or the asymmetry. The position is coherent in outline, but its load-bearing joint (adverse decisions) is under-argued, and its drafting conflicts in five places of high severity.
6. [inference] Most repairs are textual. One paragraph each fixes the two substantive gaps (A2, A3). The paper can then defend its position without changing it.

### Creative

**Reasoning chain**
1. [own knowledge] Mature safety regimes grade automation by property, not only by sector. Aviation separates advisory systems, envelope protection and autopilot modes. Medicine separates closed-loop control, such as hybrid closed-loop insulin delivery, from decision support.
2. [inference] Reframe: derive each ceiling from a few decision properties, and keep Table 1 as worked examples. The properties are harm before correction, the affected person's capacity to contest, whether the good is rationed, target type and size of decision space, and scale and speed. A short derivation rule lets readers place cases the table omits, such as fraud flags (A11) and screening triage (A4).
3. [own knowledge] Boeing's MCAS was a protective function. It contributed to two crashes (2018 and 2019), partly because pilots were not told about it and could not readily override it. Protection is not automatically safe, which supports a validated grade 3 for protective stops (A7).
4. [assumption] Asking reviewers to record a judgement before they see the recommendation reduces anchoring. If true, this targets the mechanism behind nominal grade 1 review rather than its symptom (A3). It is unverified, to be tested rather than asserted.
5. [inference] Authority could be earned through an evidence gate, staged like phased drug approval. A ceiling would rise only after a prospective comparison with the realistic human baseline, audited by subgroup, with a working appeal route. This answers what would change the paper's mind without granting authority now (A16), if the author accepts that ceilings can be revised by evidence.
6. [inference] Two cases would test the framework where it is weakest. The Dutch childcare-benefits case involved a learning system whose flags were reviewed by humans and which still failed; for an AI-specific claim, that is a stronger economic case than Robodebt. The WHO TB screening guideline is the strongest pro-automation case the paper must meet.

### Critical

**Reasoning chain**
1. [inference] The strongest counterargument is that a nominal grade 1, where a human confirms most recommendations under time pressure, may protect people less than an honest grade 2 with a properly resourced, fast appeal. The paper's ceiling could produce the first while forbidding the second (A2, A3).
2. [evidence] The paper's own source finds automation bias with imperfect decision aids, and finds that training or instructions do not prevent it (Parasuraman and Manzey abstract). EU law names automation bias for systems that recommend (AI Act Article 14(4)(b)). The safeguard the paper relies on most is the one its sources doubt.
3. [evidence] The costs fall on different people. Restraint costs people in low-resource settings, where WHO conditionally endorses software reading of chest X-rays in place of human readers, on low-certainty evidence (A4). Permission costs wrongly approved borrowers and third parties (A23), and wrongly flagged families (A11). The paper names the first trade-off (Section 6.6), but not the others.
4. [evidence] Several assumptions are strained:
   - that naming makes someone answerable, when Robodebt had accountable officials (A6);
   - that no one can answer for market harms, when Knight Capital paid a penalty (A14);
   - that human bias is heterogeneous, when shared biases exist (own knowledge, A20);
   - that military gaps cannot be closed, which is contested (A17).
5. [inference] The leading view fails under any of these conditions:
   - grade 1 review cannot be made real;
   - review capacity cannot be funded;
   - evidence shows that automated decisions with working appeals cause fewer wrongful adverse outcomes than the realistic human baseline;
   - coordination on autonomous weapons fails, and states reject the anti-personnel prohibition.
6. [inference] The paper's direction survives the strongest objections only if it does three things: states why an appeal is insufficient for adverse decisions, makes grade 1 real, and concedes what its own cases show.

### Systems thinking

**Reasoning chain**
1. [evidence] The paper models three dynamics: authority creep, the label loop and detection delay (Section 4.6).
2. [inference] The asymmetry design adds a fourth. If approvals are automated and refusals go to humans, the human queue holds only proposed refusals, which concentrates deference where it harms most (A3).
3. [inference] Because the conditions bind only at grades 2 to 4, deployers have an incentive to describe systems as advice. That moves de facto authority into the least regulated grade (A3, A12).
4. [own knowledge] A named person of record can absorb blame without having control (a moral crumple zone, Elish 2019; to verify). That weakens the institution's incentive to fix the system (A6).
5. [evidence] Rationing couples decisions: in a rationed programme, enrolling one person displaces another (Obermeyer's automatic enrolment above the 97th percentile, per the slides) (A4). Remedies are also slower than harms. Robodebt's averaging was declared unlawful by consent orders in 2019, a settlement was approved in 2021, and the Royal Commission reported in 2023 (Wikipedia). So ex post contest works on a longer timescale than the harm, which is a systems reason for A2.
6. [inference] Downstream effects:
   - No binding weapons instrument existed by September 2026 (HRW), so Recommendation 5 depends on slow coordination.
   - Pre-committed market halts already implement grade 3 rules, so the market section should describe them rather than propose them (A14).
